## About this activity

**Goal:** connect what you learned about PM2.5 sources to the place you will fly.

**Materials:** a map of the area around your launch site (about 10 km in every direction), the [AirNow Fire and Smoke Map](https://fire.airnow.gov/), and your notes.

Read [Where PM2.5 comes from](/pm25/textbook/measuring-pm25-from-the-sky/#pm25-sources) and [Why PM2.5 matters](/pm25/textbook/measuring-pm25-from-the-sky/#why-pm25-matters) first. Work on paper or in your own notes; there is no single right answer for your site.

## Part 1: Find the sources

Look at the map around your launch site and list every PM2.5 source you can find or expect: roads and highways, rail lines, industry, farms, neighborhoods with wood stoves, construction, unpaved roads, and anything else.

## Part 2: Predict

<ol class="activity-questions">
<li>Which sources would you expect to dominate on a weekday morning? Why?</li>
<li>Which would dominate in the afternoon? How might a deeper boundary layer change what you measure?</li>
<li>Open the Fire and Smoke Map. Is there smoke near you today? On a smoke day, which source would you expect to dominate, and could the smoke be above the ground layer instead of in it?</li>
<li>Which of your sources produce mostly coarse particles? Would they show up in PM2.5?</li>
</ol>

<details>
<summary>Check your thinking</summary>
<ol>
<li>Morning traffic and, in cold months, wood stoves are common morning sources. A shallow morning boundary layer keeps their emissions near the ground, so concentrations are often high.</li>
<li>Afternoon sources are often the same, but the deeper mixed layer dilutes them upward, so surface concentrations are often lower. Secondary particles, which form from gases in sunlight, can make up more of the total.</li>
<li>On a smoke day, wildfire smoke usually dominates. Smoke can be in the boundary layer, aloft in a lofted layer, or both; a balloon or a comparison with ground monitors can tell which.</li>
<li>Dust from unpaved roads, fields, and construction is mostly coarse, with a fine tail, so it adds less to PM2.5 than to PM10.</li>
</ol>
</details>
