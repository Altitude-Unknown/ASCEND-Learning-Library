## About this activity

**Goal:** this activity introduces you to the four forces acting on an aircraft in flight. You will experiment with flight control as well as weight and balance.

Work through this activity on your own or with your team, then show your results to your instructor. Write your answers on paper or in your own notes.

You need paper, a few coins (pennies work well), a paper clip, a ruler, a pencil, and somewhere to measure flight distance.

## Background knowledge

The four forces acting on an airplane in flight are lift, weight, thrust, and drag. Lift is the force that pulls an airplane up into the air. Weight is the force that works opposite lift, pulling the airplane down toward the ground. When lift is greater than weight, the airplane goes up, and when weight is greater than lift, the airplane comes down. When lift and weight are equal, the airplane neither goes up nor down, but maintains altitude. Thrust is the force created by the engines and it moves the airplane forward. Drag is the resistance force created by the airplane moving through the air, and from parasitic drag from parts of the aircraft disrupting airflow.

In this activity, you will experiment with all four of these forces as they act on a paper airplane. To begin, you must construct a basic paper airplane.

## Experiments in flight

Now that you have your paper airplane, you will make some test flights. You want to make sure you have limited the variables that might affect the results of the tests you are about to complete. Make an honest effort to do everything the same each time. Throw the plane with the same amount of force and stand in the same location each time you fly your plane. If the plane gets bent or damaged, repair it or start over with a new airplane and repeat all the steps. Try to keep drafts from open doors or windows limited, and avoid any other factors that may affect your flights.

### Thrust and drag

Thrust and drag will affect how far your airplane will fly. In this experiment you will need to keep thrust and drag as constant as possible. Drag is determined by the surface of your aircraft. You will not modify the surface except for the elevator controls in the next section. Thrust, however, will come from you. You will need to make sure you toss the airplane with the same amount of force each time you fly it, throughout this activity. This first section will allow you to collect some data on flight distance and altitude while practicing to get your “thrust” setting constant.

**Step 1.** Find an area where you can safely fly your airplane. You want to be able to measure the distance it can fly with each toss. Mark a starting line using tape, a seam between the tiles on the floor, or some other permanent mark. Make sure you always toss the plane from the same location.

**Step 2.** Make 10 test flights and record the distance the airplane flies each time.

### Lift

Lift is the upward force created by airflow over the wing. In your first experiment you will modify the pitch and roll of the airplane to affect lift. In order to do this you will make adjustable tabs on your airplane. A real airplane has pitch control on the tail of the airplane called an elevator. You will make a simple elevator by making movable tabs as shown below. On an airplane the elevator moves as one unit, so as you make your adjustments, try to make sure both tabs are either up, down, or neutral.

Elevator tabs must move in unison. One up and one down, as shown, will function like ailerons and will cause your airplane to roll. Ailerons are located on the outer wings of an airplane and move opposite one another for roll control. While roll is part of airplane control, we are not interested in roll in this experiment. You can, however, experiment with roll later to see what effect ailerons have on an airplane.

### Weight

Weight is the force that wants to pull your airplane down to the ground. Every airplane has a limit to the amount of weight that it can carry. In this next experiment you will slowly add more weight to your airplane. You will have to be careful to keep the airplane balanced. If your airplane is nose heavy it will want to dive. If your airplane is tail heavy it will want to climb first, then stop, and nose over and crash. You may have to move the weights forward or backward in order to maintain proper balance. For this test you will add pennies to your airplane one at a time and observe the flight characteristics. A paper clip is helpful to hold the coins in position. If you use the paper clip, make sure you use it all the time, as it adds weight to your total weight. If you have a small scale, you can weigh the paper clip and add its weight to your total weight.

**Step 7.** Place weights in your plane, recording the flight characteristics for each flight. Attempt to balance the weight so that your flight path is as straight and level as possible. Continue until your plane is no longer controllable.

### Weight and balance

The problems that you experienced with your model are similar to problems pilots face every day that they fly. A pilot needs to complete a weight and balance calculation before each flight. All airplanes have a center of gravity. Think of it as the balance point the airplane wants to balance on. On an airplane, that center of gravity, or CG, should be centered under the wings. Every plane will have limits as to how far forward or aft this CG can be located. As people and passengers load a plane, the CG shifts either forward or aft. It is up to the pilots to make sure the airplane is within those limits. There is also a maximum weight the airplane can carry. With the results of your experiment, make a load limit for your airplane.

**Step 8.** For this part of your experiment, maximum weight will be the weight the airplane could carry and still maintain flight to at least 50% of the average distance calculated in your baseline data. Find the maximum weight value and record it in Table 3.

The center of gravity (CG) is measured from a point on the plane called the datum. On some planes the datum is the nose of the airplane, on others it is the firewall or a similar location. On your airplane model, you will use the nose, the forward-most point, as the datum. The CG will be the distance from this point.

The **forward CG limit** is the distance from the nose of the plane (maximum forward placement of a penny) at which you can place a penny and still maintain level flight, climbs, and descents using the elevator control.

The **aft CG limit** is the distance from the nose of the plane (maximum rearward placement of a penny) at which you can place a penny and still maintain level flight, climbs, and descents using the elevator control.

#### Determine the CG for your airplane

**Step 9. Empty CG location.** To locate the center of gravity, simply balance your airplane on your fingers under the wings. Find the location where the airplane balances. Mark this with a pencil. Measure from the nose of the plane to this CG location.

**Step 10. CG forward limit.** Determine how far forward you can place a penny (or heavier coins) in your airplane and still get it to fly straight, climb, and dive using the elevator. You will need to make several flights to determine this. Once you have found the location for the coins, retest your plane to find its new balance point or CG. This will be your forward CG limit.

**Step 11. CG aft limit.** Determine how far aft you can place a penny (or heavier coins) in your airplane and still get it to fly straight, climb, and dive using the elevator. You will need to make several flights to determine this. Once you have found the location for the coins, retest your plane to find its new balance point or CG. This will be your aft CG limit.

## Key terms

<dl class="key-terms">
<dt>Center of gravity</dt><dd>The balance point of an aircraft.</dd>
<dt>CG aft limit</dt><dd>The rearmost limit that an aircraft’s center of gravity can be, and still be safe for flight.</dd>
<dt>CG forward limit</dt><dd>The forward-most limit that an aircraft’s center of gravity can be, and still be safe for flight.</dd>
<dt>Drag</dt><dd>The force that slows an aircraft down. All of the surfaces of the aircraft create drag, as does the aircraft’s movement through the air.</dd>
<dt>Lift</dt><dd>Force generated by the aircraft’s airfoil (wings on an airplane, rotor blades on a helicopter) that opposes gravity.</dd>
<dt>Parasitic drag</dt><dd>Drag caused by the surface of an aircraft and any part of the aircraft that is exposed to the moving air.</dd>
<dt>Thrust</dt><dd>Force generated by the engine of an aircraft that opposes drag.</dd>
<dt>Weight</dt><dd>The force that gravity exerts on the mass of an airplane. Weight is countered by lift.</dd>
</dl>
